A one-layer model can only look up tables of token pairs and skip-trigrams. Give it a second layer, and a head can read what another head wrote a moment before. That small change is where the paper's most interesting results begin.

The real model here is `attn-only-2l`: two layers of attention, eight heads per layer, and nothing else. We run it on 3,072 tokens of ordinary English text from WikiText.

## Why depth matters {§Two-Layer Attention-Only Transformers}

> [!PAPER] · Two-Layer Attention-Only Transformers
> [![The opening of the two-layer section: depth allows composition, composition of attention heads is the key difference between one-layer and two-layer attention-only transformers, and two-layer models become more like a computer program running an algorithm](/img/papers/circuits/p5-intro.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-intro.png)
>
> **What it says:** one intuition for the power of deep models is that "depth allows composition". "Composition of attention heads is the key difference between one-layer and two-layer attention-only transformers". Without it, a second layer would only add more heads for skip-trigrams. With it, two-layer models become "much more like a computer program running an algorithm" than look-up tables.
>
> **What it means:**
>
> Think of two clerks at one desk. The first clerk writes notes on a shared board. The second clerk reads the board before doing their own job. The second clerk can now do things that neither clerk could do alone, because the first one prepared the ground.
>
> In a one-layer model, every head reads only the token embeddings. Part 4 showed what that gives: a bigram table and a set of skip-trigrams, such as "keep … in mind". A skip-trigram is a rule of the form "if token A appears earlier and the current token is B, raise C". All of it is a fixed table that you can read from the weights.
>
> In a two-layer model, a head in the second layer reads a stream that the first layer has already changed. So what it sees is no longer a plain token. It can be "the token that came after the word I saw before". Rules about such derived facts are what turn a table into a small algorithm.

> [!DEFINITION] Composition
> Two heads **compose** when a head in a later layer reads something that a head in an earlier layer wrote into the residual stream. The later head then works on information that no single token holds.

## Three kinds of composition {§Three Kinds of Composition}

Every head reads the stream through three matrices and writes back through one. So there are three places where a later head can pick up an earlier head's work.

> [!PAPER] · Three Kinds of Composition
> [![The paragraph recalling that heads read in subspaces chosen by W_Q, W_K and W_V and write to a subspace chosen by W_O, and that heads operate on small subspaces and can easily avoid significant interaction](/img/papers/circuits/p5-small-subspaces.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-small-subspaces.png)
>
> **What it says:** every head reads subspaces of the residual stream chosen by $$W_{\mathrm{Q}}$$, $$W_{\mathrm{K}}$$ and $$W_{\mathrm{V}}$$, and writes to a subspace chosen by $$W_{\mathrm{O}}$$. Head vectors are much smaller than the stream: $$d_\text{head}/d_\text{model}$$ is around 1/10 to 1/100. So "attention heads operate on small subspaces and can easily avoid significant interaction".
>
> **What it means:**
>
> Picture the stream as a wide whiteboard and each head as a person who only reads a narrow strip of it. A strip is narrow, and the board is wide. Two people can work all day on different strips and never see each other's writing.
>
> So composition is not automatic. A later head only composes with an earlier head if its strip overlaps the strip that the earlier head writes. Training decides whether that happens. In our model $$d_\text{head}/d_\text{model}=64/512=0.125$$, which is near the top of the paper's range.

> [!PAPER] · Three Kinds of Composition
> [![The list of the three kinds of composition, Q-, K- and V-Composition, each where W_Q, W_K or W_V reads in a subspace affected by a previous head, and the paragraph saying that Q- and K-Composition affect the attention pattern while V-Composition affects what is moved](/img/papers/circuits/p5-three-kinds.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-three-kinds.png)
>
> **What it says:** there are three options. In **Q-composition**, $$W_{\mathrm{Q}}$$ reads a subspace affected by a previous head. In **K-composition**, $$W_{\mathrm{K}}$$ does, and in **V-composition**, $$W_{\mathrm{V}}$$ does. Q- and K-composition "both affect the attention pattern". V-composition affects what a head moves, and creates "virtual attention heads". "Composing movement of information with movement of information gives movement of information."
>
> **What it means:**
>
> Recall from Part 2 that a head does two jobs. Its QK circuit decides **where** to look, and its OV circuit decides **what** to carry. Q- and K-composition change the "where" job. V-composition changes the "what" job.
>
> Think of a post office. In Q-composition, a note on the parcel changes which address the clerk looks for. In K-composition, a note on each house changes which house matches. In V-composition, the clerk carries a parcel that another clerk already carried once.
>
> The last kind is the simplest. A parcel moved twice is still a moved parcel. Two V-composed heads act like one new head that moves information in one step. Q- and K-composition are richer, because a changed "where" can make attention patterns that no single head could make.

## The logits as a sum of paths {§Path Expansion of Logits}

> [!PAPER] · Path Expansion of Logits
> [![The paragraph asking how the logits are computed and saying that we write a product where every term is a layer and expand it into a sum where every term is an end-to-end path through the model](/img/papers/circuits/p5-logits-intro.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-logits-intro.png)
>
> **What it says:** the most basic question is "how are the logits computed?". As for the one-layer model, the paper writes the model as a product with one factor per layer. Then it expands the product into a sum "where every term is an end-to-end path through the model".
>
> **What it means:**
>
> Think of a river with two weirs. Water can flow straight past both weirs, or it can be diverted at the first, at the second, or at both. Every drop at the sea took one of these four routes. Count the drops on each route, and you know where the water came from.
>
> The model works the same way. Each layer either lets the stream pass or adds a head's output. The path expansion lists every route from the input tokens to the logits, so each route can be studied alone. Part 3 did this for one layer; now there are two.

Here is the paper's whole equation, with its picture of each term:

> [!PAPER] · Path Expansion of Logits
> [![The paper's equation: T equals Id tensor W_U times (Id plus the sum of layer-2 heads) times (Id plus the sum of layer-1 heads) times Id tensor W_E, which expands into the direct path, the individual head terms and the virtual attention head terms, each with a small diagram](/img/papers/circuits/p5-fig-logits.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-fig-logits.png)
>
> **What it says:** the whole model $$T$$ is a product of four factors: the unembedding, layer 2, layer 1 and the embedding. Each layer is $$\text{Id}$$ plus the sum of its heads. The product expands into three groups. The "direct path" term contributes to bigram statistics. Then come the "individual attention head" terms, and the "virtual attention head" terms, which "correspond to V-composition".
>
> **What it means:**
>
> Read the top line from right to left, the way the data flows. First the embedding $$W_{\mathrm{E}}$$. Then layer 1, where the stream either passes ($$\text{Id}$$) or goes through one of its heads. Then layer 2, with the same choice. Finally the unembedding $$W_{\mathrm{U}}$$.
>
> The bottom line is the same thing multiplied out. Each term is one route. The small diagrams show which boxes each route passes through: no head, one head, or one head in each layer.

### The derivation

The expansion is the same algebra as $$(1+a)(1+b)=1+a+b+ab$$, with matrices. Write $$H_1$$ for the heads of the first layer and $$H_2$$ for the second. From Part 2, one head is $$A^h\otimes W_{\mathrm{OV}}^h$$: its attention pattern $$A^h$$ mixes tokens, and $$W_{\mathrm{OV}}^h=W_{\mathrm{O}}^hW_{\mathrm{V}}^h$$ acts inside each token. A layer adds its heads to the stream, so a layer is $$\text{Id}+\sum_h A^h\otimes W_{\mathrm{OV}}^h$$.

So the whole model is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T={}&(\text{Id}\otimes W_{\mathrm{U}})\\
&\cdot\Big(\text{Id}+\sum_{h\in H_2}A^h\otimes W_{\mathrm{OV}}^h\Big)\\
&\cdot\Big(\text{Id}+\sum_{h\in H_1}A^h\otimes W_{\mathrm{OV}}^h\Big)\\
&\cdot(\text{Id}\otimes W_{\mathrm{E}})
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiply the two middle brackets out. You get four groups: $$\text{Id}\cdot\text{Id}$$, layer-2 heads times $$\text{Id}$$, $$\text{Id}$$ times layer-1 heads, and layer-2 heads times layer-1 heads. The mixed-product rule from Part 2, $$(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD)$$, turns each product of two heads into one term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(A^{h_2}\otimes W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_2})(A^{h_1}\otimes W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_1})\\
&=(A^{h_2}A^{h_1})\otimes(W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_2}W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_1})
\end{aligned}
$$

Now attach $$\text{Id}\otimes W_{\mathrm{U}}$$ on the left and $$\text{Id}\otimes W_{\mathrm{E}}$$ on the right, and use the same rule once more. The result is the paper's bottom line:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T={}&\text{Id}\otimes W_{\mathrm{U}}W_{\mathrm{E}}\\
&+\sum_{h\in H_1\cup H_2}A^h\otimes\big(W_{\mathrm{U}}W_{\mathrm{OV}}^hW_{\mathrm{E}}\big)\\
&+\sum_{h_2\in H_2}\sum_{h_1\in H_1}(A^{h_2}A^{h_1})\\
&\qquad\otimes\big(W_{\mathrm{U}}W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_2}W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_1}W_{\mathrm{E}}\big)
\end{aligned}
$$

Read it group by group. The first line is the **direct path**: embed a token and unembed it at once, which is the bigram table of Part 1. The second line has one term per head, in either layer, that reads the embeddings directly. These are the same skip-trigram terms as in the one-layer model. The last two lines hold one term for every pair of a layer-2 head and a layer-1 head. In each, the layer-1 head moves information, and then the layer-2 head moves the result again.

> [!DEFINITION] Order of a term
> The number of heads a path goes through. The direct path has order 0. A single-head term has order 1. A virtual-head term, through one head in each layer, has order 2.

**A toy example.** Take a stream of one number per token and three tokens, $$x_0=(1,2,3)$$. Each layer has one head. In layer 1, each token reads the token before it (token 1 reads itself), and the OV "matrix" is the number $$w_1=0.5$$. In layer 2, token 2 reads tokens 1 and 2 half each, the others read themselves, and $$w_2=2$$.

**On the real model.** Our script does the same on `attn-only-2l`, with 3,072 tokens. It keeps the attention patterns and the layer-norm scales from a normal run, and holds them fixed. Then every step is a matrix product, so the expansion is exact. It builds the direct path, the 16 single-head terms and the 64 virtual-head terms, adds them, and compares with the model:

```python
o0 = [A0[:, h] @ (x0n @ W_V[0, h]) @ W_O[0, h] for h in range(8)]       # what each layer-0 head writes
virt[h2][h1] = A1[:, h2] @ (cen(o0[h1]) / s1 @ W_V[1, h2]) @ W_O[1, h2]  # layer-1 head h2 moving what h1 wrote
full = direct + sum(o0) + sum(o1_single) + sum(all virt)                # then centre, scale and unembed
```

```text
== 2. PATH EXPANSION OF THE LOGITS ON REAL TEXT (patterns and layer-norm scales frozen)
  stream before layer 1 rebuilt from the terms: max |diff| = 5.7e-06
  logits rebuilt as direct + 16 head terms + 64 virtual heads: max |diff| from the model = 4.2e-05 (logits range -18.2 .. 25.8)
```

The 81 terms rebuild every logit to within 0.00005, on logits that run from -18.2 to 25.8. So the expansion is not an approximation. It is the model, written another way.

> [!PAPER] · Path Expansion of Logits
> [![The paragraph saying that the direct path term and individual head terms are identical to the one-layer model, the virtual attention head term corresponds to V-Composition, and virtual heads tend to not play a significant role in small two-layer models](/img/papers/circuits/p5-logit-terms.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-logit-terms.png)
>
> **What it says:** the direct path term and the individual head terms are "identical to the one-layer model". The "virtual attention head" term corresponds to V-composition. In practice, virtual heads "tend to not play a significant role in small two-layer models".
>
> **What it means:**
>
> This is a strong and useful claim. If it holds, the logits of a two-layer model look almost like those of a one-layer model. They are a bigram table plus a list of skip-trigram heads. Everything that Part 4 learned to read would still apply.
>
> But notice what the logit equation hides. Each $$A^h$$ of a layer-2 head is treated as given. Where does that pattern come from? It comes from the second layer's QK circuit, and that is where Q- and K-composition live. The next section opens it.

We test the claim about virtual heads later, in the term importance section. Our model gives a more mixed answer than the paper's.

## The attention scores as a sum of paths {§Path Expansion of Attention Scores QK Circuit}

> [!PAPER] · Path Expansion of Attention Scores QK Circuit
> [![The paragraph saying the logit expansion misses the most radically different property, that Q- and K-composition give much more expressive second layer attention patterns, and recalling that the first-layer QK-circuit is the same matrix as in the one-layer model](/img/papers/circuits/p5-qk-intro.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-qk-intro.png)
>
> **What it says:** the logit expansion "misses what is probably the most radically different property" of a two-layer model. Q- and K-composition give "much more expressive second layer attention patterns". For a first-layer head, the QK circuit is "the same matrix we saw in the one-layer model": $$C_{QK}^{h\in H_1}={W_{\mathrm{E}}}^{\top}W_{\mathrm{QK}}^hW_{\mathrm{E}}$$.
>
> **What it means:**
>
> A first-layer head scores pairs of plain tokens. Its question is fixed: "standing on token B, how much do I like token A?" That is the table of Part 2.
>
> A second-layer head scores pairs of streams that layer 1 has already changed. So its question can be about more than the token itself. For example, it can look for an earlier position whose stream says "the token before me was B". That is a kind of question no one-layer head can ask. Part 6 shows that this is exactly how induction heads work.

> [!PAPER] · Path Expansion of Attention Scores QK Circuit
> [![The paragraph saying that by the second layer the QK circuit acts on x_1, the residual stream after first layer attention heads, written as a product with the first layer on both the key side and the query side, then expanded with the path expansion trick](/img/papers/circuits/p5-qk-second.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-qk-second.png)
>
> **What it says:** in the first layer, $$W_{\mathrm{QK}}$$ acts on the token embeddings. "But by the second layer", it acts on $$x_1$$, "the residual stream after first layer attention heads". The first layer sits on both the "key side" and the "query side". "Then we apply our path expansion trick to the product."
>
> **What it means:**
>
> A score is a meeting of two vectors: the destination's stream (through the query) and the source's stream (through the key). In layer 2, each of those two streams is itself a sum: the embedding plus what every layer-1 head wrote.
>
> So the score is a product of two sums. Multiply it out, and each term pairs one route on the query side with one route on the key side. That is the same trick as for the logits, applied twice.

### The derivation

Write the stream at position $$i$$ before layer 2 as a sum of paths. It is the embedding, plus what each layer-1 head $$h$$ moved into position $$i$$:

$$
x_{1,i}=W_{\mathrm{E}}t_i+\sum_{h\in H_1}\sum_{i'}A^h_{ii'}\,W_{\mathrm{OV}}^hW_{\mathrm{E}}t_{i'}
$$

Here $$t_i$$ is the one-hot vector of token $$i$$, as in Part 2. The score of a layer-2 head $$h$$ for destination $$i$$ and source $$j$$ is $$s_{ij}=x_{1,i}^{\top}W_{\mathrm{QK}}^hx_{1,j}$$. Put the sum in on both sides. Each side has a "direct" part and a "moved by a head" part, so the product has four groups. Write $$d_i=W_{\mathrm{E}}t_i$$ for the direct part and $$m_i$$ for the part that layer-1 heads moved in:

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_{ij}={}&d_i^{\top}W_{\mathrm{QK}}^h\,d_j+m_i^{\top}W_{\mathrm{QK}}^h\,d_j\\
&+d_i^{\top}W_{\mathrm{QK}}^h\,m_j+m_i^{\top}W_{\mathrm{QK}}^h\,m_j
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term is the **no composition** group: both sides are plain embeddings, as in a one-layer model. The second is the **Q-composition** group: a layer-1 head prepared the **query**: it changes what the destination is looking for. The third is the **K-composition** group: a layer-1 head prepared the **key**, which changes what each source offers. In the last group, layer 1 prepared both.

**A toy example.** Let the query be $$q=q_d+q_h$$ with a direct part $$q_d=(1,0)$$ and a moved part $$q_h=(0,1)$$. Let the key be $$k=k_d+k_h$$ with $$k_d=(2,1)$$ and $$k_h=(1,3)$$. The four terms are $$q_d\cdot k_d=2$$, $$q_h\cdot k_d=1$$, $$q_d\cdot k_h=1$$ and $$q_h\cdot k_h=3$$. They add up to 7, and indeed $$(1,1)\cdot(3,4)=7$$.

> [!PAPER] · Path Expansion of Attention Scores QK Circuit
> [![The paragraph explaining that the expansion must be written as a 6-dimensional tensor, a (4,2)-tensor, with terms of the form A_q tensor A_k tensor W, where A_q moves query-side information between tokens and A_k moves key-side information](/img/papers/circuits/p5-qk-tensor.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-qk-tensor.png)
>
> **What it says:** the expansion has to be written as a "6-dimensional tensor", because the score is a function of two streams. Each term has the form $$A_q\otimes A_k\otimes W$$. Here $$A_q$$ "describes the movement of query-side information between tokens". $$A_k$$ does the same for the key side, and $$W$$ combines them into a score.
>
> **What it means:**
>
> The notation looks heavy, but the idea is the one in our toy. Each term needs two attention patterns, not one. One says how layer 1 moved information into the destination (the query side). The other says how layer 1 moved information into the source (the key side).
>
> In the logits, one pattern was enough, because a logit has only one position. A score has two positions, a destination and a source, so it needs one mixing matrix for each. That is why the tensor grows from two factors to three. When a side has no head on it, its factor is just $$\text{Id}$$.

> [!PAPER] · Path Expansion of Attention Scores QK Circuit
> [![The paper's QK expansion: the second-layer QK circuit as a query-side sum times W_QK times a key-side sum, expanded into the no-composition term, pure Q-composition terms, pure K-composition terms and terms with both, each with a small diagram](/img/papers/circuits/p5-fig-qk.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-fig-qk.png)
>
> **What it says:** the second-layer QK circuit is a query-side sum, times $$W_{\mathrm{QK}}^h$$, times a key-side sum. It expands into "the no composition term", terms of "pure Q-composition", terms of "pure K-composition", and terms that are "interactions between both Q-composition and K-composition".
>
> **What it means:**
>
> These are exactly the four groups of our derivation. Look at the small diagrams: the left column is the query side, and the right column is the key side. An orange box in the first layer on a side means a head prepared that side.

**On the real model.** Our script splits the scores of all eight layer-1 heads into these four groups, with the layer-0 patterns frozen. The groups add up to the model's scores to within 0.00007. Then it asks a functional question. If we delete one group, how much does the attention pattern change? We measure the change with the total variation distance.

> [!DEFINITION] Total variation distance
> A way to compare two attention rows, each of which adds up to 1. Take half the sum of the absolute differences. It is 0 when the rows are the same and 1 when they put their weight on completely different tokens. We report the mean over all destination positions.

## Measuring composition from the weights {§Analyzing a Two-Layer Model}

The paper then reverse-engineers one two-layer model. It starts with a map of which heads compose.

> [!PAPER] · Analyzing a Two-Layer Model
> [![The paragraph saying small two-layer models often have a very simple structure of composition, where the only type is K-composition between a single first layer head and some second layer heads; the first layer head attends to the previous token and the second layer heads are induction heads](/img/papers/circuits/p5-simple-structure.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-simple-structure.png)
>
> **What it says:** small two-layer models often have "a very simple structure of composition". There, "the only type of composition is K-composition between a single first layer head and some of the second layer heads". That first-layer head "primarily attends to the previous token". The second-layer heads are "induction heads".
>
> **What it means:**
>
> Of all the routes the expansions allow, the paper finds that its model uses very few. One first-layer head does a simple job: at each position, it copies in information about the token just before. Some second-layer heads then use that copied information in their keys.
>
> Why would a key want to know the previous token? Because then a source position can advertise "the token before me was B". A destination standing on B can find it. Part 6 builds the full mechanism; here we only need the fact that the composition is K-composition, with one head.

> [!DEFINITION] Previous-token head
> A head whose attention pattern puts most of its weight on the position just before the current one. It copies information about the previous token into the current position's stream.

> [!PAPER] · Analyzing a Two-Layer Model
> [![The paper's diagram of Q-, K- and V-composition between the twelve layer-0 heads and twelve layer-1 heads, with induction heads in aqua and the previous-token head in red; strong lines appear only in the K-composition panel, from the previous-token head to three induction heads](/img/papers/circuits/p5-fig-composition.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-fig-composition.png)
>
> **What it says:** a diagram with one panel per kind of composition. Each dot is a head: 12 in layer 0 at the bottom, 12 in layer 1 at the top. A line joins two heads when they compose. Only the K-composition panel has strong lines. They run from one red previous-token head to the aqua induction heads.
>
> **What it means:**
>
> This picture is a wiring diagram drawn from the weights alone, without any text. It says that, out of 144 possible pairs for each kind, only a few pairs of heads talk to each other.
>
> The paper marks this diagram with a correction. It was computed with a bug, which we come back to below. Its main message about K-composition survived the fix.

> [!PAPER] · Analyzing a Two-Layer Model
> [![The paragraph defining the composition measure as the Frobenius norm of the product of the relevant matrices divided by the norms of the individual matrices, with formulas for Q-, K- and V-Composition, and saying that the expected amount for random matrices is subtracted and that only significant K-composition with one layer 0 head is found](/img/papers/circuits/p5-comp-measure.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-comp-measure.png)
>
> **What it says:** composition is measured by "the Frobenius norm of the product of the relevant matrices, divided by the norms of the individual matrices". For K-composition, the score is $$\|W_{\mathrm{QK}}^{h_2}W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_1}\|_F/(\|W_{\mathrm{QK}}^{h_2}\|_F\|W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_1}\|_F)$$. Q-composition uses $${W_{\mathrm{QK}}^{h_2}}^{\top}$$, and V-composition uses $$W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_2}$$. The paper subtracts "the empirical expected amount for random matrices of the same shapes".
>
> **What it means:**
>
> The question is: how much of what head $$h_1$$ writes can head $$h_2$$ read? The product $$W_{\mathrm{QK}}^{h_2}W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_1}$$ is "first $$h_1$$ writes, then $$h_2$$'s key reads". If $$h_2$$'s key reads exactly the strip that $$h_1$$ writes, the product is big. If the strips are at right angles, the product is zero.
>
> Dividing by the two norms removes plain size. A head with large weights should not look more connected only because its numbers are large. The result is a score between 0 and 1 that measures alignment, not volume.

### The measure, step by step

> [!DEFINITION] Frobenius norm
> The size of a whole matrix: square every entry, add them all up, and take the square root. For a matrix with one column, it is the usual length of a vector. It is written $$\|M\|_F$$.

The score for K-composition is:

$$
\text{K-comp}(h_1,h_2)=\frac{\|W_{\mathrm{QK}}^{h_2}W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_1}\|_F}{\|W_{\mathrm{QK}}^{h_2}\|_F\;\|W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_1}\|_F}
$$

Read it left to right. On top is the size of the combined map "head $$h_1$$ writes, then head $$h_2$$ reads the result as a key". Below are the sizes of the two maps on their own. The ratio can never be more than 1, because the norm of a product is at most the product of the norms.

**A toy example.** First the norm. For the matrix $$\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&0\end{bmatrix}$$, the norm is $$\sqrt{1+4+4+0}=3$$. Now take a stream of three numbers. Head 1 reads dimension 1 and writes it, doubled, into dimension 3. So its $$W_{\mathrm{OV}}$$ has a single entry 2, in row 3 and column 1. Head 2's query reads dimension 1 and its key reads dimension 3: $$W_{\mathrm{QK}}$$ has a single 1, in row 1 and column 3.

The product $$W_{\mathrm{QK}}W_{\mathrm{OV}}$$ has a single entry 2, so its norm is 2. The norms of the two factors are 1 and 2. The K-composition score is $$2/(1\cdot2)=1$$: perfect alignment. The Q-composition score uses $$W_{\mathrm{QK}}^{\top}$$, whose query side reads dimension 3. The product is zero, so the score is 0. A third head whose key reads dimension 2 also scores 0, because head 1 never writes there.

**On the real model.** Our script computes all three scores for all 64 pairs of heads. For the random baseline, it averages the score of 200 pairs of random 512 × 512 matrices of rank 64:

## The correction {§Correction: Attention Head Composition Diagram}

After publication, the authors found a bug that affected the composition diagram.

> [!PAPER] · Correction: Attention Head Composition Diagram
> [![The correction note saying that a bug in an underlying library affected one diagram and impacts the interpretation of the two-layer section: there is more attention head composition going on in that model than it seemed](/img/papers/circuits/p5-bug.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-bug.png)
>
> **What it says:** a bug in a library for low-rank matrices "only affected one diagram", but it changes the reading of the two-layer section. "In particular, there is more attention head composition going on in that model than it seemed."
>
> **What it means:**
>
> The paper explains the bug in its correction notes. To save time, the library used a shortcut that is valid for traces and eigenvalues. It applied a similar shortcut to the Frobenius norm, where it is not valid. So the diagram did not measure the composition score as defined. It measured something narrower, which happens to respond very strongly to induction-head composition.
>
> That is why the original diagram looked so clean. It filtered out most other kinds of composition. Our scores above use the correct formula: we multiply the full matrices and take the norm of the product.

> [!PAPER] · Correction: Attention Head Composition Diagram
> [![The original and the corrected composition plots, both with the baseline subtracted. >
> **What it says:** the original plot and the corrected plot, side by side. The K-composition with the previous-token head "remains unchanged". There is more composition than before: two induction heads also rely on a head "which attends to the last several tokens".
>
> **What it means:**
>
> Compare the two K-composition panels. The thick lines from the red previous-token head are still there. Around them, many faint lines appear in every panel. The corrected world is less tidy: heads compose more than the first figure showed.

## Which terms matter {§Term Importance Analysis}

The paper chose to ignore the virtual heads because it saw no V-composition. It then checks that choice with an experiment.

> [!PAPER] · Term Importance Analysis
> [![The paragraphs saying that every individual virtual head might be unimportant while they matter in aggregate, and that ablating an implicit term that only exists if you expand out the equations is hard](/img/papers/circuits/p5-term-why.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-term-why.png)
>
> **What it says:** even if no single virtual head matters, they might "matter in aggregate". Checking this is hard, because a virtual head is "an implicit term that only exists if you expand out the equations". Running the expanded model term by term "would be horribly slow".
>
> **What it means:**
>
> Think of a choir. No single voice may be loud, but 144 quiet voices together can fill a hall. So "each virtual head is small" does not prove "all virtual heads together are small".
>
> An ordinary ablation sets a stored number to zero. A virtual head is not stored anywhere. It is a piece of a sum that exists only on paper. To remove it, you need a way to run the model without that piece.

> [!DEFINITION] Ablation
> An experiment that removes one part of a model, for example by setting its output to zero, and measures how much worse the model gets.

> [!PAPER] · Term Importance Analysis
> [![The algorithm for measuring the marginal loss reduction of nth order terms: run the model and save the attention patterns, run it again with the patterns frozen and the head outputs replaced by zeros, then repeatedly with the outputs saved from the previous run; freezing the patterns is what makes this target V-composition only](/img/papers/circuits/p5-term-algo.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-term-algo.png)
>
> **What it says:** the trick is to run the model several times, "replacing the present activations with activations from previous times you ran the model". Step 1 saves all attention patterns. Step 2 freezes them and replaces every head output "with a zero tensor of the same shape". Each later step uses the outputs saved in the step before. "Freezing the attention patterns to ground truth" makes the ablation target only V-composition.
>
> **What it means:**
>
> Watch what each run lets through. In step 2, every head output is zero, so only the direct path reaches the logits. That is the order-0 model. In step 3, each head gets the output it produced in step 2, which was computed from the direct path only. So paths through one head get through, and paths through two heads do not. Each new run allows one more head along a path.
>
> The difference between the losses of two runs is the **marginal** effect of one order. Frozen patterns keep Q- and K-composition untouched, because the patterns already contain them.

Our script does the same thing in a different way. It already has every term from the path expansion, so it just adds up the orders it wants, and unembeds the sum. With frozen patterns and frozen layer-norm scales, this gives the same partial models.

> [!DEFINITION] Loss in nats
> The model's average surprise at the true next token: minus the natural log of the probability it gave that token. A uniform guess over all 48,262 tokens has loss $$\ln 48262\approx10.78$$ nats. Lower is better.

The marginal effect of order $$n$$ is a simple difference:

$$
\Delta_n=\mathcal{L}_{\le n}-\mathcal{L}_{\le n-1}
$$

Here $$\mathcal{L}_{\le n}$$ is the loss when all terms up to order $$n$$ are kept. For order 0, the paper compares with the uniform guess. A negative $$\Delta_n$$ means order $$n$$ helps. The paper also divides by the number of terms, to get the help per term.

>

## Virtual attention heads {§Virtual Attention Heads}

The paper ends the section with a closer look at the term it decided to skip.

> [!PAPER] · Virtual Attention Heads
> [![The paragraph saying that V-Composition creates terms that act as an independent unit which performs one head operation and then the other, the composition h_2 ∘ h_1, with its own attention pattern A^{h_2}A^{h_1} and its own OV matrix W_OV^{h_2} W_OV^{h_1}](/img/papers/circuits/p5-virtual-def.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-virtual-def.png)
>
> **What it says:** V-composition creates terms that "operate as a kind of independent unit which performs one head operation and then the other". The result $$h_2\circ h_1$$ has "its own attention pattern", $$A^{h_2\circ h_1}=A^{h_2}A^{h_1}$$, and "its own OV matrix", $$W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_2\circ h_1}=W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_2}W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_1}$$. Deeper models can have higher-order virtual heads such as $$h_3\circ h_2\circ h_1$$.
>
> **What it means:**
>
> Think of a relay race. The first runner carries the baton from position A to position B, and the second carries it from B to C. Seen from the stands, the baton simply went from A to C. A virtual head is that A-to-C trip, seen as one move.
>
> The formula makes it exact. The two patterns multiply, so $$A^{h_2}A^{h_1}$$ says where the final information came from. The two OV matrices multiply too, so $$W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_2}W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_1}$$ says what was carried and how it was changed on the way. The pair has exactly the shape of one head, $$A\otimes W_{\mathrm{OV}}$$.

> [!DEFINITION] Virtual attention head
> The combination of a head $$h_1$$ and a later head $$h_2$$ that reads what $$h_1$$ wrote through its values. It behaves like one head with pattern $$A^{h_2}A^{h_1}$$ and OV matrix $$W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_2}W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_1}$$, but it is not stored anywhere in the model.

Is $$A^{h_2}A^{h_1}$$ really an attention pattern? Each row of $$A^{h_1}$$ adds up to 1, and so does each row of $$A^{h_2}$$. A row of the product is a weighted average of rows of $$A^{h_1}$$, with weights that add up to 1. So it also adds up to 1. And if neither head looks into the future, neither does the product. On the real model, the rows of 1.7 ∘ 0.7 add up to 1 to within 0.0000005, and its future entries are 0.

> [!PAPER] · Virtual Attention Heads
> [![The paragraph saying there are a lot of virtual attention heads: normal heads grow linearly in the number of layers, virtual heads of two heads quadratically, of three cubically; normal attention heads are large, with one pattern and d_head dimensions, which is unwieldy for small tasks](/img/papers/circuits/p5-virtual-many.png)](/img/papers/circuits/p5-virtual-many.png)
>
> **What it says:** "there are a lot of virtual attention heads". The number of normal heads "grows linearly in the number of layers". The number of virtual heads of two heads "grows quadratically", of three heads cubically. "Normal attention heads are, in some sense, 'large'": one pattern and $$d_\text{head}$$ dimensions. That is unwieldy for small tasks, such as noticing whether a text is in the past or present tense.
>
> **What it means:**
>
> Count them. With 8 heads per layer and 2 layers, our model has 16 heads and $$8\times8=64$$ virtual heads of order 2. The paper's model has 24 heads and 144 virtual heads. A model with 12 layers of 12 heads has 144 heads. It has $$66\times144=9{,}504$$ virtual heads of order 2, because there are 66 pairs of layers.
>
> A normal head is a big tool: a full attention pattern and 64 dimensions to copy. Many jobs need far less, such as carrying one bit like "past tense". A virtual head can do such a job with a small part of two existing heads. That is why the paper expects virtual heads to matter more in larger models, even though they matter little in its small one.

The paper's next section takes up the one kind of composition that really matters in its two-layer model: the K-composition behind induction heads. That is Part 6.

> [!TAKEAWAYS] Key takeaways
> - **Composition is what a second layer adds.** A later head can read what an earlier head wrote. Without it, a two-layer model is just a one-layer model with more heads.
> - **There are three kinds.** Q- and K-composition change where a head looks, through its query or its key. V-composition changes what it carries, and creates virtual heads.
> - **The logits are a sum of paths:** the direct path, one term per head, and one virtual head $$(A^{h_2}A^{h_1})\otimes(W_{\mathrm{U}}W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_2}W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_1}W_{\mathrm{E}})$$ per pair. On `attn-only-2l`, the 81 terms rebuilt the logits to within 0.00005.
> - **A second-layer score is a sum of four groups:** no composition, Q-composition, K-composition and both. Removing the K terms changed the pattern of induction head 1.6 by 0.75, and removing the Q terms by only 0.20.
> - **Composition can be scored from the weights** as a ratio of Frobenius norms. In our model, one pair stands out: K-composition 0.124 from previous-token head 0.3 to head 1.6, against a random baseline of 0.044.
> - **The paper's composition diagram had a bug.** The corrected plot keeps the main K-composition but shows more composition overall.
> - **Single heads do most of the work.** The paper: direct path 1.8, single heads 5.2, virtual heads 0.3 nats. Our model: 0.68, 4.04 and 1.13 nats, with the layer-1 heads giving 3.78 of the 4.04.
> - **Our virtual heads mostly move the start token.** They give 0.90 nats through the start token and only 0.07 through real tokens. For real text, the paper's conclusion holds.
> - **A virtual head is a real head in disguise,** with pattern $$A^{h_2}A^{h_1}$$ and OV matrix $$W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_2}W_{\mathrm{OV}}^{h_1}$$. Their number grows with the square of the number of layers.
